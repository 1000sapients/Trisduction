## Why there are four

A reader inside one tradition will not accept another tradition's scripture as evidence, and is right not to. Building one volume that cites all three canons and asking every reader to grant the other two is a request, not a method. So the office is read four times. Volume I is a geopolitical and archaeological audit that adjudicates no scripture. Above it the same position is read three times, once inside each corpus, by the same instrument, each reading answerable to its own canon alone. The structural and linguistic finding is the same in all three and is reached three separate times on three disjoint bodies of evidence. The structural reading stands on its own ground, and it is what this set means when it speaks of the geometry of the position: the shape of the office as the texts describe it, prior to and independent of any occupant. The scriptures are witnesses on their own.

## Who should read which volume

Four readers ask for one book each, and each is handed a different volume, because each volume admits as evidence only what its reader already grants. None of the four requires the other three. A reader who takes one and leaves the rest loses nothing of the argument, and a reader who takes all four will find one finding reached four times on four bodies of evidence that were not permitted to lean on one another.

| Reader | Volume | What it admits as evidence | What it does in the set |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The historian or scholar** | **I · A Critical History of Israel, Judah, and Their Successors** | The material and documentary record alone: text read in its original language, the royal annals and archives of the empires, the excavated ground, and the physical constraint of terrain, water, grain, and march. No scripture is adjudicated. | The ground floor. It establishes what happened, what it cost, who paid, and what the ground shows, on evidence no confession owns, so that the three readings above it stand on a floor the reader has already checked. |
| **The Jewish reader** | **II · The Conceded Throne** | The Hebrew Bible alone, enforced against the finished text by a verification gate. No Gospel, no Qurʾān, no Second Temple, Qumran, rabbinic, or later source is cited as evidence for any claim. | The reading a reader who grants only the Tanakh can check to the last verse: the rest named as the promise's terminus, given and not entered; the deficit located at reception; the monarchy conceded on demand and re-issued eight times; and the grammar of receipt, passive throughout. |
| **The Christian reader** | **III · History of the Messianic Position in the Jewish, Christian, and Qurʾānic Records** | All three canons, each read first from inside itself and none corrected by another, in chronological order: the Hebrew scriptures, the Second Temple literature, the four Gospels compared in date order, the rabbinic and medieval record, fifteen centuries of claimants, and the Qurʾān last. | The reading that follows the figure across the whole record and measures his growth stage by stage, so that a reader who holds the Christian claim can see where the specification was written, where the occupant was identified, what the Gospels say from inside about reception, and why the book stops at a boundary rather than a name. |
| **The Muslim reader** | **IV · The Office of the Messiah** | All three corpora, Qurʾān-weighted, with the Qurʾānic account read from inside the Qurʾān rather than as a gloss on another book, organised on the office's own coordinates rather than on chronology. | The reading that closes on the Qurʾānic seal: the position held apart from every occupant, the Servant sealed and the seal shown not to be the seat, the counterfeit seat named, and the occupancy held open as a verdict rather than as an absence of one. |

The four say one thing and say it four times. The position is stated with precision, offered repeatedly, and never entered. The failure is located at the receiver and never at the agent. The figure offered against that failure grows without limit while the failure stands where it stood. And the grammar by which the figure receives what he holds never changes. A reader of any one volume can test that on the corpus in hand and owes nothing to the other three.

## Naming conventions across the set

Volumes III and IV carry one form of each prophetic name, drawn from a stated list, with the honorific travelling through quotation. The honorific is a reverence convention and not an argument: it asserts no corpus's reading of any figure and decides nothing in either direction. Figures the Qur'ān does not name as prophets keep their corpus form and carry no mark, which is why Joshua, Samuel, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, and Ezra stand unmarked. Adjectival forms are never marked.

Volume II carries no honorifics at all and states why: a study admitting the Hebrew Bible alone as evidence should not carry, in its running text, a naming convention drawn from a corpus it has excluded. The suspension is a consequence of the single-corpus constraint rather than an exception to it, and nothing interpretive follows from it in either direction.

## One item left open

The set has no series title. Each volume names the other three and states the relation, which is sufficient for a reader and for a Zenodo `related_identifiers` block. A series title is an identity act and is the author's to make, not the scribe's. If one is wanted it should be settled before the first of the three is minted, because a series statement added after minting will not appear in the earlier records.
