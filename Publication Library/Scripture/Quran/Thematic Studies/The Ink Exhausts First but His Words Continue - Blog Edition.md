# The Ink Exhausts First, but His Words Continue

*What a small piece of algebra turned out to be saying, and what it does not say*

Mohammad F. Islam · 4 August 2026

Two verses run the same construction and almost nobody reads it the way it is built.

Q 18:109: say, were the sea ink for the words of my Lord, the sea would be exhausted before the words of my Lord were exhausted, even were we to bring the like of it in replenishment. Q 31:27 raises the supply and keeps the shape: were every tree a pen, and the sea with seven seas after it to replenish it, the words of Allah ﷻ would not be exhausted.

Read quickly, that lands as an attribute: *the Words are inexhaustible*. Read as written, it is not an attribute at all. It is a comparison, and the grammar puts the load somewhere else.

## Where the verb actually falls

One root carries both sides of Q 18:109. N-f-d, to be spent, used up, exhausted. The verse applies it twice.

The affirmed, indicative clause falls on **al-baḥr**, the sea. *La-nafida al-baḥru*, the sea would be exhausted. The Words appear only in the subordinate clause governed by *qabla an*, before. In Q 31:27 the Words do take the verb, but under negation, *mā nafidat*, and negation is one of the few things this register permits to be said at all.

So the verse does not assert an abstract property and then illustrate it with an image. It grants an unbounded material supply, applies one verb to two terms, and reports which term runs out. The medium is the grammatical subject of the exhausting. The Words are what the exhausting fails to reach.

That is a small observation and it changes what you are allowed to carry away.

## The sentence you may take, and the one you may not

Three positions come out of the construction and they are not of one weight.

**The medium terminates.** This is the affirmed clause. It concerns created inscription, and it is a statement in the register a created reader actually occupies.

**The Words do not.** This is received as given. It is not derived here from anything, and nothing below derives it.

**Created language inherits non-exhaustion.** This is barred. It carries a predicate past the scope it was deposited in. Ink, pens, seas, tongues, corpora, and models are media. What the verse says about the Kalam does not transfer to the vessel, and the Qur'an is blunt about vessels elsewhere: *kānā yaʾkulāni aṭ-ṭaʿām*, they both used to eat food.

The distinction is easy to state and easy to lose. The verse is not *words are inexhaustible*. It is *the ink runs out first*, with the second term left standing precisely because the verse declines to exhaust it.

## The other place the same shape showed up

I have spent the last while on a piece of foundational mathematics that has nothing to do with any of the above, and it arrived at the same shape from the other end. Here it is in plain terms, because it is small enough to hold.

Since 1946 there has been a research programme in logic that demarcates what counts as a logical notion by asking what survives a group of transformations. Mautner started it by extending Klein's Erlangen programme out of geometry and into logic; Tarski gave it its canonical form; Sher, McGee, Feferman, Casanovas and Bonnay refined it. The machinery behind it is Hilbert's finiteness theorem of 1890: for the right kind of group, the invariant functions are finitely generated, so the criterion has a finite catalogue behind it and is a criterion rather than a wish.

Ask the same question one register over, about how a claim is *decomposed* rather than about what its objects are, and the machinery will not run. The operation there is not permutation but abstraction, the setting aside of a constituent. Permutation rearranges and has an inverse. Abstraction discards, and what is discarded is not recoverable from what remains: a summary does not determine what it summarised. So the transformations do not form a group, and the theorem never gets to its first hypothesis.

Then the result goes further than I expected when I started. Suppose every constituent of a claim can be set aside. Take any function invariant under each of the three abstractions, and follow it:

> f(x₁, x₂, x₃) = f(0, x₂, x₃) = f(0, 0, x₃) = f(0, 0, 0)

Three substitutions. Every invariant function is constant. The whole invariant theory of that register is the constants and nothing else.

Which means the criterion there does not merely lack its underwriting theorem. It **demarcates nothing**. The instrument you brought does not fail to reach far enough; it collapses completely and reports one value regardless of what it was pointed at.

## Why I am putting these two things on the same page

Carefully, and with a fence.

The shape is the same. A medium is exhausted. The thing it was reaching toward is untouched by the exhausting, and the exhausting says nothing about it. Q 18:109 makes that vivid by letting the supply be as large as you like and showing it still terminate first. The algebra makes it exact by letting the instrument be a complete invariant theory and showing it collapse to a single value.

Everything else is disjoint, and I want that on the record rather than left to be assumed.

The mathematics concerns occupancy vectors under an operation performed by created readers. It quantifies over nothing else. It is not evidence for the verse. The verse is not evidence for it, and neither is a commentary on the other. If the algebra had come out the other way, the verse would stand exactly where it stands, because it was never resting on the algebra. That asymmetry is the whole reason I can print them together without one of them borrowing from the other.

What the mathematics does supply is a checkable instance, in a register a created reader can compute in an afternoon, of a structure the verse exhibits in a register no created reader enters. Seven seas of ink is a deed, and the point of the verse is that the deed can be made as large as you like and still fall short. That is worth knowing in a form you can verify, and it is worth knowing that verifying it tells you nothing whatever about the far term.

## The title, and why it is only half a sentence

*The ink exhausts first* is the part I can defend. It is the affirmed clause of the verse and it is what the theorem proves in its own register, and I can hand you both and you can check both.

*His Words continue* is the other half and I am not proving it. It is received, it is stated as received, and the honest thing is to leave the two clauses in the order the verse puts them: the one you can check, and then the one you cannot, with nothing smuggled from the first into the second.

There is a version of this essay that would have run the two together and let the algebra glow onto the theology. That version would have been more satisfying to write and would have been a fraud, because it would have taken a result about created abstraction and dressed it as a result about the Kalam. The fence is not modesty. It is the difference between a finding and a costume.

## What the small theorem actually leaves you with

One thing, and it is not the thing I went looking for.

Any procedure that decomposes a claim, screens the decomposition, and computes a verdict has to put its hardest part somewhere. The screening is a finite list. The verdict is arithmetic. Neither has room to carry what Church and Tarski say something in the chain must carry. So it sits in the decomposition, in the step where a person reads a claim and decides how to cut it, which is the step everyone treats as the part not yet automated.

It is not the part not yet automated. On this reading it is the part that provably cannot be, and calling it a temporary limitation has the typing backwards. The instrument's honest limit turns out to be the instrument's load-bearing organ.

That is a conclusion about instruments. It stays there.
